### --[ Background

I'll steal [Gera's](https://phrack.org/issues/72/phrack-prophile-on-gera) approach and have a short version and a lengthy historians
version:

When I was 8 or 9 I asked my brother "how do you crack copy protections",
and he said "it's easy, you just invert a jump". I didn't know what a jump
was, but remembered the advice. I learnt assembly at 16, then started
cracking and virus writing, then moved into reverse engineering and
hacking/vuln-dev, started training government folks on doing that to finance
my math degree, accidentally started a company, got acquired by Google when
my health was bad, recovered for a few years, got tired of security, started
another company in performance optimization, sold that, then spent a few
years caring for family. Now interested in LLM and AI stuff, because
everything is in motion and new and interesting.

---------- Version for historians and really bored readers: --------------

My dad struggled professionally in his 40s while I was on the way. Money was
tight. He answered a newspaper ad and became a developer. My 6 year older
brother bought himself a self-assembly computer at age 8 or 9 and taught
himself game programming and cracking; he sold two commercial games at age
14. He was the smart nerd in the family. I wasn't.

Dad taught me BBx BASIC when I was 8, but didn't do much with it. I typed in
magazine listings to get games, but couldn't debug them to get them to run.
I asked my brother how to crack copy protections, and he said: "It
is easy, you just invert a jump. You need to know assembly." - and that's
all I remembered. I became the artsy kid instead, drawing, and as a teen
trying my hand at editing Quake 3D models, using a software called "QuakeMe"
that had some limitation in the shareware (non-paid) version.

I went to the US for a high school exchange year. At Barnes&amp;Noble I saw
"Master Class Assembly Language" by Wrox Press and bought it. It taught me
what a JMP is, how to put the CPU into protected mode, how a DPMI extender
and paging works, and all the things I needed. I installed an IRC client and
found the cracking scene, joining #cracking4newbies (you'd get booted from
#cracking immediately if you weren't obviously good). I started learning and
cracked QuakeME with a *terrible* butcher job, but it worked. I was hooked.
I read ed!son's cracking tutorial, then stumbled into what would become
Fravia's reverse engineering website.

I had found a practically infinite fountain of puzzles and peers to puzzle
with.

The EFNet cracking scene was a very vibrant place. An entire generation of
curious kids, spanning 10-15 years age difference, congregated in shared
spaces. There was a lot of 1990s weirdness (from today's perspective toxic
or even sociopathic), but many weird kids found their communities. I met
many people that are now important in the security world there - too many to
name. Rolf Rolles, scut, acp, random_, Neel Mehta, Stephan Esser, Anders
Vogh, Solar Designer, vizions, the_grugq, the_owl, G-R0M, iceman.de, Nishad
Herath, etc. etc. etc.

The cracking scene ran on a piece of software called W32dasm, but I was an
early adopter of IDA Pro when it first became "available". Slow and
deliberate suited me, and IDA was a tool for thorough work. I used IDA
almost daily for the next 20 years (the last 10 years years it's been
perhaps twice a year). I've been an IDA user for 30 years now.

I hacked my first computer in 1996 by abusing the PHF vulnerability, a
machine at a Mexican university. I didn't know what to do from here, as I
didn't know Unix at all. I read VLAD and 29A magazine. The Win32.Cabanas
source code and article in 29A were an absolute eye-opener for me, and I
meditated on Win32.Cabanas for a long while. Solar Designer's Crackme that
included custom virtual machines and everything involved with executable
packing (PECrypt, Procdump32). I read Phrack, too, but until Smashing the
Stack, a lot of hacking articles seemed alien to me. The cracking scene was
full of assembly, interrupts, some crypto even, and many hacking articles
were "hey we social engineered our way in" and "here is a manual for some
obscure telco system". Fascinating, but far away from what I knew.

"[Smashing the stack](https://phrack.org/issues/49/smashing-the-stack-for-fun-and-profit_md)" was an eye opener to me. And writing shellcode was easy
if you were used to writing keygens in assembly. But as a cracking scene kid
I knew nothing about UNIX, or using a shell other than the terrible CMD.EXE.

I returned to Germany a changed person, deeply into the cracking scene, and
interested in the social status within that scene and all the puzzles.

I had two years of German high school to go. I earned some money cutting
sheets of metal reactants with a scissor at a firm nearby. I saw an ad that
a copy protection dongle company was looking for people to test the security
of the dongle. I applied, and then learnt during the interview process that
my future boss was iceman.de. The job was great and allowed me to work from
a cafe or a bar with a laptop, which was helpful because the relationship
between my parents had deteriorated, and with it my ability to find peace at
home.

At the time, there was a conference/party called "UC3D" - "underground
conference 3D" - which was one of the last places where the cracking scene,
the demo scene, and the hacking scene still had touchpoints (the scenes had
largely split and diverged). I took a train to Bingen in Germany and met
random and lorian, who I had known online for years. The party had 
live-on-site-demo-coding competitions, but also a "hardware compression" 
competition (where you had to crush a piece of hardware to the smallest
possible volume). I met some other youths that were dabbling in hacking and
that were talking about having compromised and networked hundreds of
machines, and there was a lot of talk about using those machines in DDoS
networks. I was disappointed by some of the "hackers" I talked to, because
they seemed uninterested in writing their own exploits (which is really all
I cared about). The party was a bit somber because the french sceners didn't
come after the ETOILENOIRE busts the week before.

Shortly thereafter a "mass exodus" from the cracking scene into the hacking
scene happened. Scut, an early TESO members also had home in cracking,
invited a bunch of crackers into TESO. It worked out great. The cracking
scene folks merged with the existing highly technical hacking folks, and for
a brief moment TESO was among the most respected "hacking groups" for sheer
technical output. ADM was quite possibly deemed cooler, but TESO was up
there, and for a brief moment we had an extremely collaborative culture. We
wrote up everything we learned in "TESO informationals", text files that
described what we were up to - many of which would've justified a conference
talk. We hung out on IRC, we exchanged literature recommendations, and
helped each other solve problems.

Near high school graduation I saw a post on Bugtraq about a stack overflow
in Netscape Enterprise Server, an important commercial webserver at the time
(it ran CNN.com, which was an important news website, culturally about as
relevant as Fox today). The people that had posted the overflow seemed to
struggle with exploitation, and I emailed them telling them I could do it.
They emailed back that if I succeeded they'd send me $1k. I sent the exploit
the next day. They offered me a job in Bangkok, and given that I was about
to graduate from high school and had a few months of downtime before
starting my replacement service for the mandatory military service, I
agreed. I was happy, because in the past year my home life had gotten very
difficult due to things outside of my control, so a path offering me a quick
escape and some money was great.

I graduated from German high school and took a flight to Bangkok the next
day. It was there that I met a few old-school hackers that had started a
security consultancy. I also met Fyodor Yarochkin. We worked together, and I
consider him a close friend and a great human being to this day. It was fun
times -- software was so full of bugs, and people were not used to buying a
pentest and getting hacked by zero days in core infra services. It was also
fun to be 18 and in a foreign land with good food and interesting work. I
remember Fyodor and me playing around with restricted x86 shellcode at the
time, and managing to do alphanumeric shellcode with '_' and '-' added
(don't ask me why I needed those characters now), and scripting IDA trying
to do static analysis using IDC. It was fun. Fyodor also got me interested
in all the strange other architectures at the time, from PA-RISC (where the
stack grows upwards) to SPARC (to this day the most beautiful assembly).

I returned to Germany to start the military replacement. It was in a home
for troubled youth in a very rough part of town. The job gave me about 2
hours each morning to hack on my own stuff before the kids arrived.

While in Thailand I had agreed with a fellow windows hacker, Joey__, that we
should meet. He was one of the few people at the time that wrote Windows
exploits (I can really just remember a handful of nicknames: darkspyrit 
(Barnaby Jack), Joey__, a Japanese person that called himself UNYON / dark
penguin (?) that later worked at EEye ... and a bit later Greg Hoglund (?).
Everybody kinda knew (of) each other.

Joey__ had hired two cracking sceners that I valued extremely highly, and
they had set up shop in Sri Lanka, and there they had done magical things to
extend SoftICE, the kernel debugger every windows exploit person lived in.
SoftICE is a hard thing to explain to people nowadays, but it was a software
kernel debugger that largely inserted itself *under* the OS, with limited
understanding of the actual operating system, and which would freeze the
entire system. You'd hit CTRL-D, a black window would pop up, and the entire
OS, including the clock etc. would stop. It was great for providing
extremely fine-grained control and the ability to single-step etc., but this
was before virtualization on x86 was a thing, so you'd go into SoftICE and
your music playback would hang on the last sample, and your machine clock
would stop, and literally your entire system stays put until you single step
again. And if you were deep in some tricky self-modifying code, you didn't
really dare go out of it. Anyhow, these friends of mine had reverse
engineered the (closed source) SoftICE to an enormous extent and then
written third-party extensions to it, from the practical (using SoftICE as a
tracer to unpack packed PE files) to the whimsical (adding a below-OS-level
MP3 player so you could continue listening to music while you're debugging).

I had always admired Joey__ and we agreed submit to the same Blackhat
conference so we could meet. I submitted, and got accepted, and somehow
Joey__ didn't submit -- I'm hazy on the details -- but through this I ended
up presenting at Blackhat Amsterdam 2000 on reverse engineering. Afterwards
I was asked if I could talk more about this. I was 19, fresh out of
high-school and needed a way to finance myself (I was not going to allow
myself to be dependent on my parents, given the difficult situation at
home).

I started doing reverse engineering and exploit development trainings at 
Blackhat in order to finance my studies.

Here I got into contact with all the usual three-letter suspects. In my
first class, an experienced "DoD" reverse engineer (with like 13 or 15 years
experience doing reverse engineering, an unimaginable timespan for a 19 year
old) sat in the class with some junior folks and I was confused why they'd
send someone with that pedigree to a class like mine, taught by a kid that
taught himself to reverse just a few years ago. The person was extremely
nice, and explained that he's mainly there to check out how I'm doing
things, but that he doesn't have time to train all the people that need to
learn this stuff.

Contact with the US intelligence sector was a bit unsettling at first -- I
had grown up in a *deeply* pacifist core family (both my parents saw WW2 as
children, and both had deep emotional scars from it - which led them to
embrace pacifism to an extreme extent, in spite of a very prussian and quite
martial family history). So everything to do with the military, with
espionage, and with state- to-state conflict was deeply suspicious to me,
and more so to my father. I was introduced to a few folks that had just left
the agencies -- I met Dave Aitel, and some of his former colleagues, and I
realized that a lot of these folks were intelligent, resourceful, and often
quite introspective about the moral and ethical dimensions of their work.
This soothed my instinctive antipathy.

TESO suffered from leaks, and people that got their hands on the exploits 
created a bit of havoc. This led to incidents, like FBI folks asking me and
another TESO member to have a chat over coffee in Vancouver while we were
attending CanSecWest. I still have the pin they gave me somewhere. "We
don't mind you two, but you have some really antisocial friends" is a quote
I remember vividly, and having to explain to them that Burneye (the ELF
cryptor) wasn't so much designed to annoy forensic investigators but rather
a desperate attempt at controlling exploit leaks better.

TESO's previously free-wheeling internal research culture nosedived, and
everybody tried locking stuff down more. The leaks did not stop (perhaps
some of the infra was compromised), and this got so bad that TESO members
would often only learn of the research of another TESO researcher *after*
that research had gotten leaked and published.

[There is a lesson I learnt from this: In many organizations with extremely
locked-down information flow, the only person that has a holistic view into
what the organisation is doing is the successful attacker, and that there is
a crossover point where information compartmentation kneecaps the defenders.

I have seen that lesson play out repeatedly throughout my career - in a most
egregious case, a friend of mine worked at a company defensively and wanted
access to some internal documentation. He couldn't get it, but some other
friend of mine that had not worked at that company got that documentation by
monitoring a (likely Chinese) APT exfil box.]

I had to decide on a subject to study at university. I tried law (family
tradition), but a centralized body assigned university seats for subjects in
high demand, and they assigned me to a city I didn't want to move to; I'd
have to wait two semesters to study law in the city where my then-gf lived.
First day of the semester I need to pick a subject. I went to the only
subject that didn't have people queuing for it: Mathematics, with the intent
to do two semesters to teach myself how to think clearly. I had also read
Schneier's "Applied Cryptography" and thought some math would be useful.

For the first time in my life a subject was genuinely hard for me, and I 
was middling, if not in the lower half of students. The first lecture opened
with the Prof saying "look at your neighbor to your left and to your right.
If you're here by Christmans, they won't be.". I was too proud to quit, so 
I slogged on, and eventually had the realization that if I ever wanted to
be just an epsilon above useless, I'd have to decide to love it. I did, and
I still love it.

[In my first Analysis lecture, I met my friend Soeren, who was (and is) the
best C++ developer I have ever known, and who I had the pleasure of hiring
for my company later, and worked with at Google. I loved conversations with
experienced C++ developers, because their gripes about the sharp corners of
their language would directly inform my code auditing and vulnerabilty
hunting strategies. That said, I love chatting with Soeren about most topics
until this day, even if (or perhaps because?) we often disagree.]

9/11 happened a month or two into my first year at uni. Everything went
crazy and complicated. The GWoT started, and my view of US actions had to
become more nuanced very quickly, in particular in the run-up to the second
Iraq war. I still earned my money doing trainings.

I was studying mathematics and struggling with it, only to fly to Las Vegas
or other places at regular intervals to teach a class full of people much
older than me (and often in government roles) on reverse engineering and
vulnerability development. It was very schizophrenic, and a tiny bit
isolating. [I also had the strangest conversations with my professors when I
had to tell them "hey, I cannot take the final exam at the prescribed data
because my livelihood depends on me being gone *that week*, can I take the
re-exam for people that are sick for the first one?".

I remember feeling encouraged about completing my math degree when one of my
professors told me "young man, you might make an OK mathematician if you'd
just cut out all this computer stuff".]

In 2003 Microsoft published a bunch of patches for the Windows DCOM system
after the hacking group LSD had reported a bunch of Windows remotes. The
In order to mitigate the risks MS wanted to have the patches available but
provided no information about the vulnerabilities.

I got annoyed by having the patched executables but no way to understand
what the bugs were that we patched. Microsofts release executables were
shipped with something that is called PGO nowadays (basic block reordering
based on profiling data -- Microsoft even had a patent on this stuff), and
their patches weren't -- so any byte-level comparison was out because
everything had gotten shuffled.

The fact that all the bytes were shuffled but the graph structure had
remained stable for almost all functions led me to build the first prototype
of BinDiff.

Near the end of my Vordiplom (roughly equiv to undergrad, but not a
stand-alone degree) I took a cryptography seminar. The topic I was assigned
was hash functions, and the book was Stinson's "Cryptography: Theory and
Practice". I found the chapter on hash functions inscrutable, but I had read
MD5 *implementations* during my code auditing and of course been exposed to
hash functions when cracking. So I decided to switch the literature from
Stinson to Dobbertin's MD4 collision paper, not knowing that that paper had
a reputation of being hard to read. I presented my part of the seminar, and
it was the only time in my undergrad that I did well. The Prof took me aside
after the seminar, and told me: "Listen, I see your grades and how you're
doing here. And I see how you did on this seminar. My recommendation to you
is: Go do something that *really* interests you for a change."

I had heard that Dobbertin had just become a Professor in Bochum, and I
decided to switch universities with the goal of writing my master thesis on
MD5. I thought at the time that the construct looked shaky enough to have a
go at it. I'd have a semester off beforehand. I decided to go to Argentina,
where many friends were working for Gera's company, CORE.

Those 2-3 months were some of the best in my life. Not particularly healthy
\- I'd take Spanish classes in the morning, then sit in the CORE office
working mostly on my own stuff and chatting with friends that worked at
CORE, then go home to hack on stuff with my flatmate, sleep on a matress in
an otherwise bare apartment (that initially did not even have a chair or
table; we got one when our backs started hurting), and repeat. I drank way
too much Mate and slept way too little, not to mention lack of exercise. But
I will forever cherish the mornings in Belgrano, near the Subte station of
Congreso di Tucuman, where we'd order orange juice and tostados mixtos for
breakfast after a nuit blanche spent hacking on stuff.

BinDiff really became useful, and I hacked up a website for the company
around it. I flew to LA from Argentina to teach a training class on patch
analysis. There was demand.

[My flatmate made me understand the extreme importance of exploit
reliability. He was the personality type that'd spend 3 months on
stabilizing a LPE (an unthinkable time investment at the time) to make sure
it'd always always work. I love him as a dear friend to this day.]

This Microsoft PCT SSL security patch hit while I was in Argentina, and it
was BinDiff's first "big moment" -- the tool pinpointed the fix, and within
24h of patch release there was a working and reliable exploit in CORE
Impact, allowing remote access to any unpatched IIS installation with SSL
enabled. [I was never a fan of "regular" shellcodes, so I had adapted a
friends all-assembly PE loader into a shellcode (you'd give it a PE file and
an anonymous blob of +x memory, and it would load the PE file there and call
the entry point). This allowed me to write all my post-exploitation stuff in
C/C++ and just load DLLs into the remote memory space. It had the benefit
that you'd never touch disk with any of your tools, and forensic memory
acquisition wasn't very common yet (this was 2004 after all).

The SSL PCT exploit was so reliable that it allowed loading DLLs over and
over again; I remember abusing it and eventually managing to lock up the
remote process after having loaded 30+ DLLs into the other process (I never
really implemented unloading).]

Good times. It was here in Argentina that I first learned about Gera's heap
visualization work that he had done for the dtlogin exploit, and it was an
epiphany for me. [Over the decades I have written and re-written large-scale
heap visualizers 3 or 4 times, and I had to resort to GPU acceleration to
deal with all those rectangles as early as 2006 or so. I still cannot
imagine doing any sophisticated exploitation without a good heap visualizer.
Perhaps it's time to write a Phrack article with an implementation about
one?]

I returned to Germany and moved to Bochum, into a new flat, in early summer
2004. In August 2004, Wang et. al. published their MD5 collision, and then
visited Bochum. I was invited into the discussions with Dobbertin and his
grad students and Mrs Wang. This was extremely interesting, validating but
also disappointing: My hunch about MD5 fragility had been correct, but now
I'd need to work on something else.

Unfortunately, Prof Dobbertin fell ill shortly after the semester started,
mostly disappeared from University life and eventually died of cancer. I was
stranded in Bochum, the the reason why I was there gone, and I had to wait
for the Cryptography department char being re-appointed. At the same time 
people wanted to buy BinDiff, and I set up a German GmbH to sell it (people
had asked for all sorts of source code escrow etc. to buy from me as an 
individual, once I had a legal entity all these questions went away).

(Modest) revenue started coming in, but enough to reduce my travel schedule
for trainings. And a short while after, Rolf Rolles contacted me - he had
manually decompiled the BinDiff plugin binary to re-compilable source code,
and wanted to see if he could work with me on the tool. [I had known Rolf
from the #c4n channel, and held his work in high esteem - but the fact that
he had decompiled BinDiff blew my mind. We came to an agreement on how to
collaborate, and Rolf made many important contributions to the core BinDiff
algorithms. While him and me parted ways in an unfriendly and ugly manner a
few years later, I still hold him in the highest regard - technically, there
are few people that can match his depth of expertise, his persistence, and
his vision.]

I also was introduced to Ero Carrera, who was working at F-Secure at the
time and had also looked at using the graph structure of malware to perform
malware clustering. He also joined the "company", and we ended up renting an
office.

We were initially called SABRE Security ("security analysis by binary
reverse engineering"), and the website had grand plans: BinDiff, BinNavi,
and BinAudit, three products that we had planned. We planned to finance the
development for each step from the money from the previous step.

The roadmap was: Use BinDiff revenue to build a tool that allows input
crafting (one of my motivations for studying MD5 was that I wanted to build
better solvers for the type of equation systems that come up in code
analysis, mixing boolean arithmetic with mod 2^32 or mod 2^64 arithmetic) to
reach a given point in a program (BinNavi), and to use the money from that
to build BinAudit, a hypothetical bug detection program that would identify
security issues in binaries. Over the 7 year history of the company, we
never started actual real development on BinAudit, but we did build BinDiff,
and BinNavi, including the REIL IR that was supposed to be used to feed into
the solvers.

SABRE (later zynamics) was, in retrospect, an insanely hubristic project. 

[We had planned to do BinDiff, all of z3/KLEE, and then a hypothetical
strong static analysis tool on binaries (like Codesurfer/x86?), all without
external funding and bootstrapped on training revenues. In 2008 or so I
thought about raising funding and realized that our business plan was
essentially "we will solve a bunch of fundamentally hard CS problems and
then sell the results to 1000 people at $800 a pop". I have to smile with a
sort of benign grandfatherly smile at my naive ambition and overconfidence
of my youth.]

We had rename to zynamics because we got threatened with a trademark
lawsuit. We had rather interesting customers that were often unable to talk
to us, and fielded support requests such as the following:

<PRE>
 Them: "How does BinDiff work on architectures you don't support?"
 Us: "Can you tell us the architecture, we'll support it?"
 Them: "No."
 Us: "Can you give us a list of architectures, one of which may contain
      yours, and we support all of them?"
 Them: "No."
 Us: "Uhhhh."
</PRE>
We also had to deal with many suspicious customers that wanted to drop by
our office and pay in cash, and misc. other madness. We ended up having the
company secretary do basic background checking of our customers. The stories
I could tell.

[A surprising customer segment we didn't know about were car tuners, and
that amused us to no end. We also got to do contract reverse engineering for
a few government-run attacks involving worms and rootkits that would only
get publicly discussed a few years later. We had front-row seats for
Stuxnet, front row enough to notice the body odours of the actors involved.]

zynamics was fun, we grew to 10-12 people, but it was also deeply unhealthy
for me. I ran myself ragged: Every time our money in the bank would dip
below my safety margin I'd go and sell extra trainings to make up for the
shortfall, then sales would recover and I'd still have to do the trainings,
while trying to finish my degree(s) (first my MSc, then I embarked on a
PhD). So developed stress-induced immune failure -- I got shingles, whooping
cough, and a bursitis in short sequence, with the bursitis spiking
inflammation markers beyond all reason. My doctor eventually told me: "Young
man, we are seeing symptoms in you that we normally see in 80 year old men
with immune failure. Whatever you are doing to yourself, if you want to see
the age of 35, you need to stop doing that."

I took a vacation and started surfing, and decided I need a way out of the
treadmill. This was around the time of the Wikileaks madness. Julien Assange
spent a good amount of time next to our zynamics tent at the dutch hacking
camp eating from our barbecue and discussing, and during my "vacation" I had
my car broken into, all my papers stolen, and when I drove back through
Paris my drink got spiked -- but somehow in those crazy years all of that
seemed par for the course.

Ero and me started scheming about potentially finding a buyer, initially in
the antivirus industry; we got very lucky that the Chinese Gov got caught
hacking Google at just the right time.

So after a year of ridiculous regotiation between us and mighty Google (11k
people, growing rapidly) we sold zynamics on the 28th of Feb 2011. Everybody
got a good outcome (although pocket change by today's standards), and the 
entire zynamics team transitioned into Google where we got to scale up our
compute (we had struggled with computational cost for some of our malware
work prior to that).

I joined Google - it was an exciting place in 2011 - and Google made me a
much better computer scientist but a much worse reverse engineer (just
because the nature of my work shifted a lot). [It was good, though, my boss
at Google was one of the best human beings I know, and fundamentally
committed to viewing security problems through a system architecture lens
(rather than a bolt-on product).] My involvement in vuln-dev shifted more
theoretical -- I had had the important "insights" into the proper way to
define what Sergey Bratus called a weird machine in the late 2000s, and at
Google I then began thinking about scale ("if I can fill DRAM with
Javascript bytecode, how many ads do I have to buy until a random cosmic ray
bitflip will corrupt *some* laptop out there and give me control").]

I was very happy to be able to collaborate with Mark Seaborn when Flippy the
Laptop with terrible RAM was found and sparked Mark's successful run to his
Rowhammer exploit. My contribution to this was largely figuring out that you
can do 2-sided hammering by staring at some data visualisation, and also the
idea of filling memory with PTEs (although iirc someone else had suggested
that shortly before me, so I will only claim partial credit here). Mark did
all the real work.

I started getting tired of security, took a sabbatical, finished the weird
machine paper (which was a bit of a slog, but important) and returned into
an IC role at Project Zero. P0 was full of awesome human beings, and being
in a research group like that felt somewhat liberating. [When you're
managing the build-out of a piece of software, the work is very
interlocking, like an orchestra where everybody has to be very precise.
P0-style research is more like a jazz jam, there's more room for
improvisation.]

I got more and more frustrated with security. I also have always admired
people that had multiple different careers, from my favorite applied
mathematicians to people like Gera. So after sitting next to Jann Horn and
learning about eBPF by watching him, I decided it is time to do something in
performance land, and quit Google to start a performance optimization
company.

I got together with a vuln-dev friend of mine, Sean Heelan, with whom I had
discussed a lot during his PhD work. We started the company, and ran it for
a short 2.5 years during COVID. We raised funding this time, from a16z,
built the world's first multi-runtime eBPF based continuous production
profiler, and got acquired by Elastic. It was a short run, but fun - somehow
a fantastic team assembled itself, we did great stuff, and then everything
was over before we could really blink twice. Unfortunately, my mother fell
into a coma the day the deal closed, and then died after 9 weeks of ICU
later, and I had small kids etc, so the aftermath was rather difficult for
me.

On the positive side, Elastic open-sourced our code and donated it to OTel,
so chances are that if you are running a continuous profiler these days that
you are running something based on our code. So optimyze didn't end up the

And ... well, it's 5 years later now. LLMs are now a thing and are reshaping
everything, including security, and I find myself back looking at some
security stuff, because things are changing rapidly enough for everything to
be *interesting* again. As Hunter S. Thompson said: When the going gets
weird, the weird turn pro, and I'm here for the ride, at least momentarily.

### --[ What is your favourite bug/exploit?

Each one has their own little story. They end up like pets.

One of my favorites was a RealServer bug where RealServer would take a
request URL and break it up into parts, splitting the string at '/', and
putting a sequence of char* into an array of char* on the stack - so you'd
overflow the stack buffer with char* to your own data, and you'd overwrite
EIP with that pointer, so it was really "send the right string and watch it
get executed". I remember Dave Aitel being confused about accidentally
executing code while fuzzing *without doing* anything. Gorgeous bug.

Cisco PIX had a funny bug in IKE where you could just pretend you had
already passed the key exchange phase when you hadn't, and the
implementation would start HMAC'ing your data, meaning copying it into the
buffer for MD5Update - except that the pointers in that buffer had never
been initialized. So if you managed to pollute the stack properly, you'd get
a "write your data anywhere" primitive.

When the /GS mitigations came in, the compilers would add stack canaries to
many functions. But there is a funny (obvious, but not really documented)
interaction between modern C++ programming and stack canaries: The stack
canary is only active at the *end* of a function, but most functions receive
pointers to stack-allocated C++ objects further up on the stack. The net
result of this is that if you have an unbounded stack overflow of the type
GS tries to mitigate, chances are you can overflow long enough to hit a
vtable higer up on the stack. Adobe Reader had such a bug, and you got
control on the last call before the GS check. Tremendously gratifying.

Anything ever where you get to corrupt some sort of loopy bytecode.


### --[ Will mitigations eventually make exploitation impossible?

I think properly implemented fine-grained memory tagging can make the vast
majority of memory corruption bugs impossible or at least fiendishly hard to
exploit. That said - incentives are usually aligned against security, and it
is not unlikely that we will simply never get ubiquitous memory tagging in
all devices; things like "how do you do memory tagging in a heterogeneous
compute environment where many different devices hack around in the same
shared memory" are not always clear-cut.

I don't think we have a much better mitigation on the cost/benefit tradeoff
currently. Time will tell.

To be perfectly honest, I have oscillated on this. I was doubtful about the
future of buffer overflows as early as the 30th October 2000, and then as
time passed I got very optimistic about their future.

There will be other bugs, less powerful perhaps, that will still be worth
exploiting.


### --[ Would you recommend newcomers to contribute to open source projects?

I recommend everybody contributes to open source. Spreading source code
makes us all richer. I also understand that economics can dictate not
sharing the source code -- so there is no moral judgement if you don't --
but I generally think that sharing code when you can is a fundamentally
altruistic move that makes everybody better off.


### --[ Your opinion in the infosec scene now vs then?

We have all gotten much older ;) and we can now talk about health issues as
much as bugs. Middle-aged mutant ninja turtles indeed.

We need to recall that the 90s were a weird time, geopolitically, as some
serious scholars seriously considered "the end of history" and that we'd all
just trade and be happy, a form of capitalism-meets-hippie-ideals. 9/11 put
an end to that, and the aftermath gave everybody stark reminders what power
and steel and torture means.

I share some of [Gera's](https://phrack.org/issues/72/phrack-prophile-on-gera) concerns about "mercenaries everywhere", I don't
really like the militarization of everything. Then again, this might just be
the way the world works: Any sufficiently useful technology will be co-opted
by power and used in war. The early days of manned flight are a good
example. We went from the Wright Brothers to WW1 dogfights in 16 years, and
to the massacre at Guernica in 35 years, carpetbombing Germany's industrial
centers in 40 years. Of course the structure and personalities of pilots
changed through that. And those that were around when "flight" meant
"running down a beautiful grassy you flap your arms to perhaps get 300m of
lift", seeing jet fighters bomb civilians will always (and in some sense
rightfully) seem like a terrible betrayal of the craft.

Everyone is much more specialized now - the early 2010s still had people
that could operate accross the full spectrum, from vuln-dev to persistence
to doing actual hacking. This is getting rare, people are very much in their
niche. Perhaps AI models provide some ability to become "well-rounded"
again, as they can help skill up in foreign areas.

I also think the scene is under harsher economic pressures. When there is no
money, the weirdoes that everybody says have no "common sense" congregate
and find joy. Then money arrives, and it changes both the dynamics but also
the composition of the group.

So everything is more professional, more structured, more organized, more
employer-driven, people worry about career trajectories, and they expect to
have a professional life in security. That's a very different vibe than a
bunch of kids that were thrilled that anyone would pay them for opening IDA,
and that could still avoid thinking about the consequences of everything,
and that had no expectations any of this would last more than a few years.

To finish: I used to joke that there are less than a dozen reasons why
working in security is fun, and I know most of them personally. Then over
time I had to increase that number, and nowadays I could probably name 3
dozen or more people whom I have gotten to know through security that I hold
in high esteem. So while perhaps the percentage of people I like has gone
down, the overall number has gone up, and since I am good at avoiding people
I don't like, my life is a bit richer with the scene today than it was with
the scene back then, by sheer numbers.

There are how many conferences per day now? I like some of them. The best

### --[ Reflections

Hacker Spirit:
Boundless curiosity, a desire to understand even if people say "no need to
look at that", and a true desire for experimentation and learning by
absorbing a topic and not just reading about it. As long as we can kindle
curiosity and generous sharing of what we learn, the hacker spirit is alive.
It's science, in the most basic, child-play, curiosity, joyful way.
Experiment, learn, and explain.

Exploit Industry:
You don't argue with the existence of rain. You decide what level of
exposure you can tolerate, and you act according to that. The only person
that can judge whether you can still look into the mirror tomorrow is you.
Make your 8 year old self proud, and make your 80 year old self proud.

Early Programming: 
See above.

Career and Burnout:
In a career, you need to balance exploitation (earning money) with
exploration (doing things that fill your heart with joy). I saw people burn
out because they focused on exploitation too much. I always swung back and
forth between tool building and vuln-dev, and I would not have been happy
with only one of them. And even with that I eventuall did burn out. So I
went and did something else for a while. The world is full of marvels, and
if you're willing to go deep and understand and be helpful and kind, there
is life outside of security if you ever need it.
There's a helpful analogy: Your life is a long car ride, and each of the
aspects of "you" is a passenger. You need to find a way to keep them all
happy, and that begins by listening to them, and not locking one of them
into the trunk for years.


### --[ Insights

Important principle:
As a child I understood the "canary" principle: Enforcement of rules is
often by making an example of the most egregious offender. If you're
breaking the rules, don't be in the first line. And if the first line gets
hit, hit the ground and stay there for longer than reasonable.

Hacking Milestones:
I recommend "not getting arrested, sued, or seriously harmed" is pretty high
on the list to achieve before you're 25. "Make decisions to make your 8 year
old self and your 80 year old self proud" is the milestone for 40. After
that, be aware that you're sitting in failing machinery that needs serious
maintenance.

Nontraditional Hacking:
Chiptuning of cars was entertaining. The demoscene was entertaining. The
game cheating scene had some very strong technical folks. Do something new.
Build something with your hands that nobody else has.

The "Art" of Hacking:
Finding unexpected uses of something, either for beauty, for fun, or actual
utility / gain.
